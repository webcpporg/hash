[#hash-crypto]
== Use case: a cryptocurrency wallet

A wallet app holds keys that move money, and its mistakes are paid in coins
that no one can return. Its hashes are not chosen: each chain's standards name
them, and a wallet that computes a seed, an address or a checksum differently
from every other wallet sends funds where nobody can reach them. This chapter
computes the three steps every Bitcoin and Ethereum wallet takes, from the
standards' own test vectors: the seed from the recovery words, the
hash of a Bitcoin public key in its address, and an Ethereum address with its
checksum.

Each step is a protocol's (BIP39, BIP141, EIP-55), and is shown as what a
domain library, webcpp::bitcoin or webcpp::ethereum, would build from hash's
primitives; hash has no BIP, EIP or address function. JavaScript wallets do
the same with paulmillr's noble libraries: the Ethereum library
https://github.com/wevm/viem[viem] computes Keccak-256 with `@noble/hashes`,
and https://github.com/paulmillr/scure-bip39[scure-bip39] derives BIP39 seeds
with it. The code below is webcpp's own.

[#hash-crypto-bip39]
=== The seed of a wallet: BIP39

*The problem.* A wallet must be recoverable from something a person can write
on paper. BIP39 encodes 128 to 256 random bits as 12 to 24 words, with a
checksum; the wallet then needs a 64-byte seed from the words, from which
every key derives (BIP32).

*The idea.* The seed is PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-512 of the words, normalised to
Unicode NFKD, with the salt `mnemonic` followed by an optional passphrase, at
2,048 iterations. The passphrase makes a second wallet from the same words:
someone who finds the paper without the passphrase finds an empty wallet. The
word list, the checksum and the normalisation are webcpp::bitcoin's; the KDF
is hash's `pbkdf2`.

[listing]

The words, the passphrase `TREZOR` and the seed are BIP39's first English
test vector.

*Mistakes.* 2,048 iterations do not make a weak secret strong: the words carry
128 bits or more of randomness, which is the protection, and a passphrase
alone, as a "brain wallet", is guessed. Skipping the NFKD normalisation gives
another seed for any word or passphrase with accents. And the seed is as
secret as the words: it never leaves the device in clear.

[#hash-crypto-hash160]
=== A Bitcoin address: hash160

*The problem.* A Bitcoin address must be short, and must not reveal the
public key until the coins are spent.

*The idea.* Legacy (P2PKH) and SegWit version 0 (P2WPKH) addresses commit to
hash160 of the public key: RIPEMD-160 of SHA-256 of its 33 compressed bytes.
The two digests together give 20 bytes, and an attacker would have to break
both to substitute a key. The address's encoding (Base58Check or Bech32) is
webcpp::bitcoin's.

[source]
----
include::{examples}/bitcoin_hash160.cpp[tag=example,indent=0]
----

[listing]
----
include::{examples}/bitcoin_hash160.expected[]
----

The public key is that of the secret key 1, the generator point of secp256k1;
its hash160 is the one inside the well-known address of that key, and the
example's value is computed independently with Python.

*Mistakes.* Hashing the uncompressed key (65 bytes) gives another, valid but
different address; wallets must agree on the form. RIPEMD-160 alone is legacy:
use it only inside hash160, never for a new design (<<hash-legacy>>).

[#hash-crypto-ethereum]
=== An Ethereum address: Keccak-256 and EIP-55

*The problem.* An Ethereum address is 20 bytes, typed or pasted as 40
hexadecimal digits. A single wrong digit sends funds to an address nobody
holds.

*The idea.* The address is the last 20 bytes of the Keccak-256 of the 64-byte
public key (x and y, without the prefix byte). EIP-55 adds a checksum without
changing the address: each letter of the hexadecimal form is written in upper
case where the same position of the Keccak-256 of the lower-case form is 8 or
more. A wallet that receives a mixed-case address checks the case and refuses
a typo: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-55[EIP-55] puts the chance
that a mistyped address still passes at about 0.0247 percent.

[listing]

The public key is again that of the secret key 1; the address is the one every
Ethereum tool gives for it, and the example's values are computed
independently with a Python Keccak.

*Mistakes.* SHA3-256 in place of Keccak-256 (<<hash-keccak>>) gives an address
that exists nowhere. Accepting an all-lower-case address without a checksum is
allowed by EIP-55, but a wallet should warn: it carries no protection against
typos.
